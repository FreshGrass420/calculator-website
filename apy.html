📈 APY Guide

APY (Annual Percentage Yield) is the real annual return on a savings account or investment after compounding is taken into account. Banks advertise APY rather than the simple interest rate because it shows what you actually earn in a year.

The APY Formula

APY = (1 + r/n)^n − 1

  • r — nominal (stated) annual interest rate as a decimal
  • n — number of compounding periods per year (365 daily, 12 monthly, 4 quarterly, 1 annually)

APR vs APY

APR is the simple annual rate without compounding. APY includes compounding, so it is always equal to or higher than APR. A savings account quoting 5% APR compounded monthly actually pays a 5.116% APY — the number you should compare between banks.

Worked Example

A 5% nominal rate compounded monthly gives an APY of 5.116%. A $1,000 deposit would earn $51.16 in one year. Using the rule of 72, your money would double in roughly 72 ÷ 5.116 ≈ 14 years.

How Compounding Frequency Changes APY

At a 5% nominal rate, more frequent compounding produces a slightly higher effective yield:

Compounding APY Interest on $1,000
Annually 5.000% $50.00
Quarterly 5.094% $50.94
Monthly 5.116% $51.16
Daily 5.127% $51.27

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is APY (Annual Percentage Yield)?

APY (Annual Percentage Yield) is the effective annual rate of return that accounts for compounding interest. Unlike simple interest rate, APY reflects how often interest is applied to your balance.

What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the simple annual interest rate without compounding, while APY (Annual Percentage Yield) includes the effect of compounding. APY is always higher than or equal to APR when compounding occurs more than once per year.

How does compounding frequency affect APY?

More frequent compounding results in a higher APY. Daily compounding yields more than monthly, which yields more than annual compounding, because interest is applied to the balance more often.
